HRDF-MER: Hierarchical feature refinement and cascaded dynamic fusion for multimodal emotion recognition
Jianjun Lei, Zhenmei Mu, Ying Wang
Chongqing University of Posts and Telecommunications
Published in Computer Speech & Language, 2026

ABSTRACT
Multimodal Emotion Recognition (MER) is challenged by modality misalignment, shallow temporal cue
modeling, and inefficient fusion. This paper proposes HRDF-MER, a framework that integrates hierarchical
refinement and cascaded dynamic fusion for more robust emotion recognition. To improve cross-modal
alignment and unimodal representation, HRDF-MER introduces a novel Hierarchical Cross-modal Feature
Refinement (HCFR) strategy, which integrates Cross-modal Adaptive Alignment (CAA) and Hierarchical
Feature Enhancement (HFE). The CAA module employs multi-head cross-attention to construct hierarchical
correlation matrices for precise acoustic-text alignment, and the HFE employs a Transformer with cross-modal
residual connections to further enhance unimodal representations for robust feature learning. We further
propose a Cascaded Multimodal Dynamic Fusion (CMDF) strategy, where a cross-attention encoder captures
fine-grained inter-modal dependencies and a gated fusion unit adaptively weights modalities to progressively
produce highly discriminative multimodal representations. Moreover, a multi-objective training scheme is
proposed to jointly optimize feature alignment and classification by integrating Cross-modal Label Contrastive
Loss (CLC Loss) with cross-entropy loss. Extensive experiments on the IEMOCAP and MELD datasets
demonstrate that HRDF-MER significantly outperforms state-of-the-art models, while ablation studies further
confirm the effectiveness and necessity of each proposed component.

1. INTRODUCTION
Emotion recognition is fundamental for human-computer interaction, as it enables nuanced communication,
affective understanding, and adaptive responses in complex social systems. Although unimodal approaches,
particularly those based on speech, have achieved significant progress, they remain vulnerable to environmental
noise, individual variability in expression, and contextual ambiguities, which can substantially degrade
performance. To address these limitations, Multimodal Emotion Recognition (MER) has emerged as a
promising paradigm, leveraging complementary information from multiple modalities, such as audio, text, and
vision, to achieve more accurate, robust, and context-aware affective analysis.

However, developing effective MER systems remains challenging due to the inherent heterogeneity of
modalities, as acoustic and textual data exhibit significant differences in temporal resolution, feature
representation, and semantic abstraction. Existing methods often rely on simple alignment mechanisms, such
as single-layer attention, which fail to model hierarchical emotional cues spanning low-level prosody to
high-level semantics. In addition, most fusion strategies adopt static or manually designed weighting schemes,
lacking the capacity to dynamically adjust modality contributions according to context-specific discriminative
capacity.

This paper proposes the HRDF-MER framework, which integrates hierarchical feature refinement with
cascaded dynamic fusion. The framework addresses three critical aspects: cross-modal alignment, intra-modal
representation learning, and multimodal fusion. Key contributions:
- The HCFR strategy integrates two core modules: (1) the CAA module, which utilizes multi-head
  cross-attention to establish hierarchical cross-modal alignment; and (2) the HFE module, which employs
  cross-layer residuals to mitigate intra-modal noise, suppress feature redundancy, and strengthen unimodal
  discriminability.
- A cascaded multimodal fusion module CMDF, which employs iterative cross-attention and a gating-based
  adaptive mechanism, resolving complex inter-modal dependency modeling and progressively integrating
  multi-level emotional information to produce robust and highly discriminative multimodal representations.
- A multi-objective hierarchical training framework, jointly optimizing CLC loss and cross-entropy losses.
- Extensive evaluations on the IEMOCAP and MELD benchmarks demonstrate that HRDF-MER achieves
  significant performance gains over strong baselines, while ablation studies further validate the individual
  and collective contributions of each component.

Against the rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) and multimodal large models (e.g.,
GPT-4V, LLaVA), leveraging large models for emotion recognition has become a prominent research direction.
These methods capitalize on large models' robust semantic comprehension and context modeling capabilities.
However, large model-based approaches have notable limitations: they incur heavy computational overhead,
hindering deployment in resource-constrained scenarios; additionally, their internal feature representations and
decision-making processes lack interpretability.

3. METHOD
3.1 Overview
The proposed HRDF-MER framework consists of five core components: embedding, refinement, fusion,
classification, and multi-objective optimization. (1) The embedding layer extracts initial speech and text
features via pre-trained encoders; (2) the feature refinement module aligns modalities through cross-modal
contrastive learning and enhances emotion-relevant features; (3) the feature fusion module employs a
two-layer Transformer to integrate the refined features into a unified multimodal representation; (4) the
classifier compresses fused features with max pooling and predicts emotion categories through a multi-layer
feedforward network; and (5) the multi-objective optimization jointly leverages cross-modal label contrastive
loss and inter-modal distance constraints.

3.2 Hierarchical Cross-modal Feature Refinement (HCFR)
The HCFR module consists of two cooperatively optimized components: CAA and HFE. The CAA module
establishes fine-grained inter-modal mappings to bridge distribution gaps, while the HFE module strengthens
intra-modal representations based on the aligned features.

3.2.1 Cross-modal Adaptive Alignment (CAA)
The CAA module employs a parallel modal encoder architecture to capture complementary emotional
representations from speech and text. For the text modality, the pre-trained RoBERTa-base model is used to
obtain embeddings X_e_t of dimension 768. For the audio modality, dual features are leveraged: pre-trained
Wav2Vec2-base embeddings X_e_a for semantic context, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) processed
MFCC features X_e_m for acoustic details, where the MFCC features are extracted using the librosa toolkit.

A Multi-Head Cross-Attention (MHCA) layer establishes fine-grained associations among X_e_a, X_e_m, and
X_e_t. The audio representation X_e_a is used as the query, text as key and value; the output of the MHCA is
added to X_e_a followed by layer normalization to produce the aligned acoustic representation. This whole
procedure is called the Cross-modal Attention Encoder (CAE). The same procedure is applied for MFCC
features. This CAA module employs a two-stage refined alignment architecture to progressively integrate the
two speech modalities with the text modality.

3.2.2 Hierarchical Feature Enhancement (HFE)
The HFE framework progressively enhances feature learning through multi-stage interactions using parallel
multi-head Self-Attention Encoders (SAEs) applied independently to text, aligned audio, and aligned MFCC
features. Because self-attention alone is insufficient for capturing high-level emotional semantics, an
intra-modal cross-attention mechanism is introduced that integrates information from different hierarchical
layers within the same modality: current-layer features serve as queries and lower-layer features as key-value
pairs. This cross-layer interaction injects high-level semantic constraints while retaining original information,
suppressing noise and enhancing discriminative capacity.

3.3 Cascaded Multimodal Dynamic Fusion (CMDF)
The CMDF module is a progressive cross-modal interaction architecture that employs a hierarchical attention
mechanism to integrate semantic-acoustic features. CMDF adopts three parallel Cross-modal Attention
Encoders (CAEs) that generate refined cross-modal interaction representations, alternately using text and
{audio, MFCC} features as queries. Stacking two CAE layers is empirically validated to effectively enhance
hierarchical cross-modal feature fusion: using fewer layers (0 or 1) leads to insufficient cross-modal
interaction and reduced accuracy, while increasing to three layers causes a slight performance degradation
due to information redundancy or over-smoothing.

Because MFCC features are more sensitive to timbre while audio (Wav2Vec2) features preserve richer
prosodic and rhythmic information, a cross-attention mechanism enables mutual reinforcement and calibration
between the two acoustic features. Meanwhile, a self-attention mechanism is applied to the text modality to
mitigate interference from redundant acoustic information and refocus on the text's intrinsic semantic
structure. Ablation shows that replacing this self-attention step with cross-attention, or removing it entirely,
causes a 1.5-2.0% performance drop in overall accuracy.

A gating-based fusion mechanism adaptively selects and integrates the most informative components from
each modality. A learnable gating unit generates adaptive weights (via a sigmoid) to determine the importance
of enhanced vs. cross-modal features, and the gated fusion combines them via element-wise weighting. This
gating is applied in two stages to further refine feature selectivity and suppress residual noise. The refined
features from all modalities (text, audio, MFCC) are concatenated into the final fused emotional representation.

3.4 Multi-objective Learning
The training objective combines standard Cross-Entropy (CE) loss with a multi-layer Cross-modal Label
Contrastive (CLC) loss: L = delta * L_CE + lambda * sum_{l=1..3} L_CLC^(l), where delta and lambda balance
the contributions. The CLC loss is computed at three hierarchical stages: (1) after the feature embedding
layer, (2) after the CAA module, and (3) after the HFE module, enforcing stage-wise cross-modal semantic
consistency.

The CLC loss uses learnable multi-perspective pooling: for each modality, K learnable masks aggregate the
sequence into K representation vectors, which are projected, normalized, and concatenated into a global
representation per modality. Cross-modal similarity is computed between these global representations (e.g.,
audio-to-text) via a temperature-scaled dot product, and a label-aware InfoNCE-style contrastive loss is
applied: samples sharing the same emotion label are treated as positive pairs. The bidirectional losses (e.g.,
text-to-audio and audio-to-text) are averaged for symmetric alignment, and the per-stage CLC losses are
averaged across modality pairs.

4. EXPERIMENTS
4.1 Datasets
IEMOCAP: approximately 12 hours of dyadic conversations from 10 professional actors across 5 sessions.
Four basic emotions are considered: Neutral, Anger, Happy (including Excited), and Sad, resulting in 5531
valid utterances.

MELD: derived from the TV series "Friends", contains over 13,000 utterances from multi-party conversations,
covering seven basic emotions (Anger, Disgust, Sadness, Happiness, Surprise, Fear) plus Neutral. MELD is
officially partitioned into training (9989 utterances), validation (1109 utterances), and test (2610 utterances)
sets. The dataset is heavily class-imbalanced: Neutral dominates with 6435 of 13,707 total utterances, while
Fear (358) and Disgust (361) are the rarest classes.

4.1.3 Training details
HRDF-MER is implemented in PyTorch and trained on an Nvidia Tesla V100 GPU. Zero-padding is applied
within each batch, aligning sequences to the longest in that batch. The Adam optimizer uses an initial learning
rate of 3e-3. The model trains for 50 epochs with batch size 12, using gradient accumulation with step size 4
(effective batch of ~48). The model contains approximately 250 million trainable parameters. The
hyperparameters delta and lambda are fixed at 0.5 and 0.2 respectively, found via preliminary experiments to be
optimal. For IEMOCAP, a leave-one-session-out 5-fold cross-validation strategy ensures speaker independence.

4.1.4 Evaluation metrics
For IEMOCAP: Unweighted Accuracy (UA) and Weighted Accuracy (WA). For MELD: Weighted Accuracy
(WA) and Weighted F1-score (WF1), following the official train/val/test split. An Average (Avg) score over
the metrics is also computed.

4.2 Results
On IEMOCAP, HRDF-MER achieves UA=78.64%, WA=77.66%, Avg=78.15%, outperforming baselines such
as MSMSER, KBCoAtt, SAMS, REA, SWG-MMIN, MSER, MPLMM, HBF-MRC, and MFGCN.

On MELD, HRDF-MER achieves WA=64.76% and WF1=63.02% (Avg=63.89%), exceeding baselines including
DSAGCN (WA=60.9, WF1=58.7), HI-IMC (WA=61.7, WF1=60.8), SACCMA (WF1=59.3), GCCL (WA=62.82,
WF1=60.28), REA (WF1=61.7), and MSE-Qwen-1.8B, a large-language-model-based fusion approach
(WA=62.34, WF1=59.89). HRDF-MER's 2.42% WA improvement over MSE-Qwen-1.8B is attributed to the
CMDF module's dynamic adaptability compared to MSE-Qwen-1.8B's single-layer large-model fusion.

4.3 Ablation studies
Unimodal results on IEMOCAP: text alone achieves the highest average accuracy (68.92%), significantly
outperforming audio alone (63.74%) and MFCC alone (60.40%), indicating text is the dominant modality.
Combining audio and MFCC reaches 66.92% average. Concatenating all three modalities (text, audio, MFCC)
without the proposed refinement/fusion modules reaches 75.21% average.

Removing CMDF causes the largest performance drop (Avg 76.42%, down from 78.15%), confirming CMDF's
critical role in dynamic weighting and integration of aligned features. Removing HFE causes a 0.65% drop, and
removing CAA causes a 0.42% drop, confirming the necessity of multi-scale feature learning and precise
temporal alignment respectively.

For the three-stage CLC loss, single-stage utilization yields limited improvement, while combining multiple
contrastive losses steadily enhances performance; using all three stages together yields the largest performance
leap.

4.4 Hyperparameter study
The optimal combination is delta=0.5, lambda=0.2, achieving WA=78.64% and UA=77.66% on IEMOCAP.
Extreme values (delta=1.0, lambda=0.0, i.e. CE loss only; or delta=0.0, lambda=1.0, i.e. CLC loss only) both
perform worse, showing the two loss terms are complementary.

4.5 Visualization and case study
t-SNE visualization shows that removing CMDF results in highly dispersed feature distributions and blurred
class boundaries, while retaining CMDF leads to tighter clustering of same-class features. Removing HCFR
results in more dispersed distributions especially for neutral emotions.

Confusion matrix analysis: on IEMOCAP, HRDF-MER achieves high recall for Anger (0.869) and Sad (0.838);
the main confusion is between Happy/Excited and Neutral. On MELD, due to severe class imbalance, minority
emotions (Fear, Sadness, Disgust) are prone to misclassification, with the model tending to over-predict the
majority Neutral class; for example, Fear is misclassified as Disgust 26.4% of the time. Joy is often confused
with Neutral (21.9%) and Surprise (10.5%).

A case study shows that a model with only HCFR misclassifies a sample as "Neutral" with confidence 0.6136,
relying excessively on textual features while ignoring audio cues. The full model with both HCFR and CMDF
correctly identifies the sample as "Sad" with improved confidence 0.7325, demonstrating CMDF's role in
dynamically adjusting modal fusion weights based on input features.

5. CONCLUSION
HRDF-MER is a hierarchical framework tackling modality misalignment, shallow temporal cue modeling, and
inefficient fusion in multimodal emotion recognition. The HCFR module combines CAA and HFE to achieve
precise cross-modal alignment and progressively refine unimodal representations. The CMDF strategy captures
fine-grained inter-modal dependencies via cross-attention and adaptive gating. A multi-objective training
scheme ensures robust joint optimization of feature alignment and classification. Despite these strengths, audio
data remain sensitive to environmental noise, and minority or semantically close emotions remain challenging
to recognize accurately. Future work will focus on robustness in noisy environments, integrating visual
modality information, and exploring advanced alignment and fusion techniques.
